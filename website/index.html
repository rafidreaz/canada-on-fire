Canada on Fire

Seven decades of wildfire patterns, causes and extremes, read from 448,447 fire records in the Canadian National Fire Database.

Why this data

Wildfire is now Canada's most visible environmental problem, and 2023 was the largest season on record. The headlines rarely show the longer pattern. We used the Canadian National Fire Database (CNFDB), maintained by the Canadian Forest Service, Natural Resources Canada: every reported fire since 1950 with its location, date, final size and cause, plus mapped burn perimeters from 1972 to 2024.

Each chart below makes one point and answers one of four questions:

  1. Is the area burned each year increasing, and how unusual was 2023?
  2. How do lightning- and human-caused fires differ in when, where and how big they burn?
  3. Do a few very large fires account for most of the area, and has the typical fire grown?
  4. Where are Canada's wildfire hotspots, and have they shifted?
Q1

Trend and the 2023 season

Fewer fires, extreme seasons

The number of fires reported each year peaked in the early 1990s and has fallen since, a clear decline. Area burned does not follow a straight line: it has no statistically significant trend since 1980, but it swings hard, and the two largest years on record are recent. 2023 burned 17.6 million hectares, twice any other year, and 2025 burned 8.7 million.

Q2

Lightning vs people

Two causes, two different problems

People start most fires, but they start them where they can be reached and put out. Lightning starts fewer fires, in remote forest in midsummer, and those are the fires that grow.

Q3

Extremes

A few huge fires do almost all the burning

Most fires stay small: 69% of fires with a recorded size are under one hectare. The area burned comes from the rare fires that escape, and since 1980 the escaped lightning fires have been getting larger.

Q4

Geography

A stable belt that burns harder, and further west

Fifty years of burn scars trace one belt of boreal forest from Yukon to Quebec. Its latitude has not changed: the area-weighted centre of burning stayed between 57.7 and 59.7°N in every decade. What changed is how much burns, and where along the belt. Mapped burning per year doubled in the 2020s, and the centre moved about 370 km west. Scrub through the decades to watch the scars accumulate and the centre move.

Conclusions

What the data says

Q1

Fewer fires, extreme seasons

Fire counts have fallen since 1980 (p < 0.001). Area burned has no significant linear trend, but the two largest years on record are recent: 2023 (17.6 Mha, with a record May, burning in five jurisdictions at once) and 2025 (8.7 Mha). 2023 was not an isolated spike. Evidence: Figures 1–4.

Q2

Different causes, different fires

Human fires are frequent, spring-time and close to people. Lightning fires are fewer, midsummer and remote, and cause 84% of the area burned. Evidence: Figures 5–9.

Q3

The tail is the story

Fires of 200 ha or more are 5.3% of fires but 98.9% of the area. Since 1980 the yearly median escaped lightning fire has risen (Spearman ρ = 0.68, p < 0.001); human fires show no significant change. Evidence: Figures 10–12.

Q4

A stable belt, burning harder and further west

The belt has not moved north, but mapped burning per year doubled in the 2020s and its centre moved about 370 km west. Per unit of land, the Prairie provinces burn hardest. Evidence: Figures 13–15.

Method and limits

How we built it

Data

  • CNFDB point data (448,447 fires, 1950–2025) and burned-area polygons (1972–2024), Natural Resources Canada, Open Government Licence – Canada.
  • Province boundaries: Natural Earth (public domain).

Cleaning decisions

  • Dropped year codes of −999 and 171 sparse pre-1950 records.
  • Each fire is placed in the province or territory where it burned (a spatial join), not by the agency that reported it.
  • Yearly totals match NRCan's published NFDB summary within 0.6% in every year except 1979.
  • Unknown causes are kept and shown in green, never dropped.
  • Fire sizes are often recorded at 0.1 ha, so size trends use fires of 1 ha or more.

Statistics and limits

  • Trends are tested over 1980–2025, when reporting is complete: Kendall's τ with a Theil–Sen slope, and Spearman's ρ for yearly medians.
  • New Brunswick reports from 1980 and PEI from 2018; the 2020s are a partial decade.
  • Trends are associations, not causes: suppression policy and satellite mapping changed over the period.
  • Cause is Unknown for 4% of the area burned.

Tools

  • Python: pandas, Matplotlib and Seaborn for the printed figures; Plotly Express for this site; GeoPandas for the maps.
  • Every figure is reproducible from one notebook (canada_on_fire.ipynb), which holds each figure's full code.
  • Code: github.com/rafidreaz/canada-on-fire (branch moskhan).
